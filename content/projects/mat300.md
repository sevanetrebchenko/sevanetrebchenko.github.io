*C# · math · DigiPen MAT300 · solo · January to April 2021*

MAT300 (Curves and Surfaces) at DigiPen was seven projects, each a small 2D C# WinForms app for one family of curves. To show them here I ported them into a single C++ editor with the same algorithms. Each section below starts from a curve that works, finds where it breaks, and moves on to the curve that fixes it.

## Bézier curves
A Bézier curve is defined by a handful of control points and evaluated with de Casteljau's algorithm: linearly interpolate between each pair of neighbouring points at t, then between those results, and so on until a single point is left. That point is on the curve, and the intermediate polygons (the shells) fold down onto it.

![A Bézier curve with its de Casteljau shells as t sweeps from one end to the other](/projects/mat300/bezier-shells.mp4 "de Casteljau's algorithm: the grey shells fold down to the point on the curve at t")

The same curve can also be evaluated directly, as a sum of the control points weighted by Bernstein polynomials. Both give the same curve; de Casteljau's version is the more stable of the two, since it only ever takes convex combinations of points.

Two problems show up as soon as a curve has more than a few points. Every control point pulls on the whole curve, so a high-degree Bézier curve averages its points away: it gets lazy, flattens out and never comes near the points in the middle.

![Points added one at a time in a zigzag, the Bézier curve flattening and staying away from the inner points](/projects/mat300/bezier-lazy.mp4 "adding points raises the degree, and the curve gets lazier with each one")

And the control is global: moving any one point moves the entire curve, not just the part near it.

![One control point moving up and down, the whole Bézier curve following it](/projects/mat300/bezier-global.mp4 "one point moves, the whole curve moves")

## Midpoint subdivision
Instead of evaluating the curve at many values of t, a Bézier curve can be drawn by subdivision. Splitting the control polygon at t = 0.5 gives the control polygons of the curve's two halves, and the de Casteljau shells are exactly those points. Recursing a few levels gives polygons that converge on the curve quickly, without choosing any sample points at all.

![A Bézier curve drawn by midpoint subdivision, the depth stepping from 1 to 7 and back](/projects/mat300/subdivision.mp4 "midpoint subdivision, depth 1 to 7 and back")

The depth here is fixed. A better version would stop splitting each piece once its control polygon is flat enough, so straight stretches stop early and tight bends get more detail.

## Interpolation
A Bézier curve only passes through its end points. To pass through every point, the project builds an interpolating polynomial in Newton form, from a table of divided differences. It does pass through every point, but a polynomial of high degree through evenly spaced points rings: it swings wildly between the points near the ends (Runge's phenomenon).

![Points clicked in to form a step, the Newton polynomial swinging far off the plot](/projects/mat300/newton-step.mp4 "a Newton interpolating polynomial through a step: it rings")

A cubic spline fixes that. Instead of one polynomial of high degree, it joins cubic pieces with matching position, slope and curvature at each point, solved as one linear system in the truncated power basis. Through the same points, it stays calm.

![The same step clicked in, a cubic spline passing through it without overshooting](/projects/mat300/spline-step.mp4 "a cubic spline through the same step")

Neither one is local, though. Moving the middle point of a wave, the polynomial whips across its whole length; the spline handles it far better, but the ripple still reaches every piece.

![The middle point of a wave dragged up and down, the Newton polynomial whipping along its whole length](/projects/mat300/drag-newton.mp4 "Newton polynomial")
![The same point dragged, the cubic spline rippling along its length](/projects/mat300/drag-spline.mp4 "cubic spline")

Placing the points closer together towards the ends (Chebyshev nodes) would tame the polynomial's ringing, and the spline could be solved as a small tridiagonal system rather than a dense one.

## B-splines
B-splines give up passing through the points in exchange for local control. The curve is built from basis functions that are each non-zero over only a few knot intervals, so a control point only affects the part of the curve near it.

![One coefficient of a B-spline swinging up and down, only the nearby part of the curve moving](/projects/mat300/bspline-local.mp4 "one coefficient moves; only its neighbourhood follows")

The project evaluates B-splines three independent ways: de Boor's algorithm, which is de Casteljau's idea carried over to knots; divided differences of truncated power functions; and Cramer's rule, which is slow but makes a good cross-check. All three draw the same curve.

![A B-spline function whose coefficients keep moving while the evaluation switches between three methods](/projects/mat300/bspline-methods.mp4 "de Boor (yellow), divided differences (cyan) and Cramer's rule (coral): one curve")

## Knots
The knot sequence shapes a B-spline curve without adding control points. Repeating the end knots clamps the curve to its first and last control points; repeating a knot in the middle lowers the continuity there, pinching the curve towards a control point; spreading the knots back out smooths it again.

![A cubic B-spline curve seen from above with a knot ruler below it: the ends clamp, a middle knot triples and pinches the curve, then both release](/projects/mat300/knots.mp4 "clamping the ends, then tripling a knot in the middle")

The algorithm doesn't care about dimension, either: the same de Boor evaluation runs on points in 3D.

![A clamped B-spline helix in 3D with its de Boor stages drawn, the camera orbiting around it](/projects/mat300/orbit-3d.mp4 "the same B-spline curve in 3D")

The cubic spline from this project also became the path in my [CS460](/projects/cs460/) character animation project.

## What I'd do differently
- **Flatness-based subdivision** instead of a fixed depth, as above.
- **Chebyshev nodes** for the interpolating polynomial, and a **tridiagonal solve** for the cubic spline.
- **Knot insertion** (Boehm's algorithm), to refine a B-spline without changing its shape, and **NURBS**, to draw exact conics.
