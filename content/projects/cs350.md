CS350 (Advanced Computer Graphics II) at DigiPen was a series of projects on one OpenGL framework: a deferred renderer, a threaded scene loader, spatial partitioning with an octree and a BSP tree, and collision detection with GJK. The scenes here are the Amazon Lumberyard Bistro, about 2.8 million triangles in 1,321 objects, and the Stanford dragon for a closer look at the trees.

## Loading the scene

Scene geometry is loaded using a pool of worker threads.
The file is split into chunks at object boundaries, each chunk placed into a pending queue for worker threads to pull from.
Each worker returns a payload containing the parsed vertex attributes.
Vertex normals are computed for smooth shading, and vertices are indexed to avoid storing duplicate data.
The main thread assembles the geometry data of each chunk to match the expected SoA vertex layout in the shaders and uploads it to buffers on the GPU for rendering.

![The Bistro street streaming in around a fixed camera](/projects/cs350/loading.mp4 "The Bistro street streaming in around a fixed camera")

Without hardcoding the slowdown for pacing the clip above, the entire Bistro scene, composed of 2.84 million triangles across 1,321 objects, loads in around 0.26 seconds.
The San Miguel 2.0 scene, containing 9.98 million triangles and 1,004 objects, loads in about 1 second.
The scene geometry is streamed in across multiple frames so that the window remains responsive to input, avoiding the wait at startup and making the framework much nicer to work in.

Uploads are ordered by distance from the camera so that closer objects load in first.

## Rendering techniques

This project was my first introduction to building a deferred renderer.
Each frame was composed of two main passes: a geometry pass for writing the G-buffer, and a lighting pass for shading the visible pixels.
The G-buffer contains separate attachments for positions, normals, depth, and material properties.

![The Bistro street lit by a warm key light, a violet fill and an orange accent](/projects/cs350/gbuffer-output.jpg "the lit output")

![The G-buffer's view-space positions as colors](/projects/cs350/gbuffer-position.jpg "view-space position")
![The G-buffer's normals as colors](/projects/cs350/gbuffer-normal.jpg "normals")
![Depth in greyscale, near in white and far in black](/projects/cs350/gbuffer-depth.jpg "depth, log scale from 2.5 m (white) to 25 m (black)")

Because only the visible pixels make it to the G-buffer, lighting calculations for occluded geometry can be skipped entirely.
This makes deferred rendering significantly more performant for scenes with many lights, as the complexity scales linearly with the number of lights (as compared to O(number of lights * number of objects) in a traditional forward renderer).

For this project, I implemented the Blinn-Phong lighting model.
Most surfaces in the scene use the same ambient and diffuse coefficients, so the G-buffer attachments containing the material properties are largely a single color and not that interesting.
Transparent objects, such as the windows of the bistro, are not supported.

To render foliage, the fragment shader samples the alpha channel of the object's base color texture and discards any fragments whose alpha is below 0.5.
This texture is retrieved from the object's material properties and bound when rendering each object.
Fragments that pass write their position, normal and material properties to the G-buffer like any other surface.
As a result, each leaf is cut to the shape of its texture instead of showing up as a flat quad.

## Spatial partitioning

For this project, I implemented two methods of partitioning scene geometry.

### Octree

An octree recursively splits space into eight cells at a time, subdividing where geometry is dense and stopping early where it's sparse.
Triangles that straddle a cell boundary are clipped using the Sutherland-Hodgman algorithm and triangulated so that each triangle belongs to exactly one cell at a time.

![The lit dragon with octree cells shown one level at a time, each level in its own color from coral at depth 1 to yellow at depth 5](/projects/cs350/octree.mp4 "one level every 1.25 s, each in its own color")

A cell stops subdividing when it contains at most 2,000 triangles.
This threshold is intended to balance the two phases of collision detection.
Lowering the threshold leaves fewer triangles to test, but increases the depth of the tree (and the cost of traversing the tree during the broad phase).
Raising the threshold makes the tree shallower, but increases the number of triangles in leaf nodes and results in a more expensive narrow phase.

The octree for the 871,000-triangle Stanford dragon contains 2,729 total cells across 6 levels.
The Bistro's octree is more complex, with 15,145 total cells across 13 levels.

### BSP tree

A BSP tree partitions space into two half spaces using a randomly oriented split plane.
Each plane is chosen from a set of random candidates, scored by how evenly it divides the triangles of the parent space.

![The dragon cut into regions by planes, first one split at a time and then a level at a time, each region filled with its own flat color](/projects/cs350/bsp.mp4 "the first 15 splits at 1.25 s each, then one level per step down to depth 8; each region has its own flat color")

In the video above, the first 15 splits are shown one at a time to give a better idea of how the algorithm works.
For the clarity of the visualization, the orientation of each split plane is selected so that each one perfectly aligns with the camera.
In reality, the planes may be oriented arbitrarily in 3D space.

Using the same subdivision limit of 2,000 triangles, the BSP tree for the Stanford dragon contains 1,891 total cells across 10 levels.
The Bistro's BSP tree contains about 7,900 total cells across 20 levels.

Compared to the octree, the BSP tree has more levels but fewer total cells, since each split only divides the input space in two.

## Collision detection

Collision runs in two phases.
The broad phase traverses the levels of the octree to find the leaf nodes a moving object overlaps with, and the narrow phase runs GJK against the triangles in those leaves.

![A ball thrown into the Bistro, the octree cells it passes through drawn around it, zooming in on the hit](/projects/cs350/gjk.mp4 "a ball thrown into the scene; after the hit, only the leaf it landed in and that leaf's parent cells stay drawn")

I chose to use the octree for collision detection as it is simpler to visualize.
it decides whether two convex shapes intersect by building a simplex inside their Minkowski difference, and stops as soon as it either encloses the origin or proves it doesn't.

## What I'd do differently

The parallel scene loading system is intentionally simple.
There are a lot of optimizations that can be made, such as eliminating the locking contention by replacing the mutex used for synchronizing worker threads with a lock-free queue, or preallocating one VBO large enough to store the entire scene and uploading to it at incremental offsets (instead of a separate buffer per object).
The loading logic can easily be extended to use higher mesh LODs of objects that are beyond a certain distance from the camera, further improving load times and reducing the amount of VRAM required to keep the entire scene in memory.

Clipping triangles at cell boundaries guarantees that each triangle belongs to exactly one leaf node, but it increases the total number of triangles in the scene.
For the Stanford dragon, the triangle count grows by 27% in the octree and 39% in the BSP tree.
The Bistro is worse, since large triangles straddle split boundaries many times.
Its 2.84 million triangles become ~6.39 million in the octree and ~5.72 million in the BSP tree.

For collision detection, triangles don't need to be split at all.
This was originally done for the project to be able to easily isolate the geometry of the cell that the ball collided with.
If I were to implement this again, I would have leaf nodes reference overlapping triangles by index instead of clipping them.
This approach would no longer grow the number of triangles in the scene.
Collision queries would need a way to tag triangles that have already been tested, as a triangle that crosses a split boundary is referenced by several leaf nodes at once.

Building the tree is also an operation that is well suited to be parallelized.
Once a node is split, each child owns the list of triangles and doesn't share any data with its siblings, so each subtree can be built as an independent task.
